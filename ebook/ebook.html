AI foundations for marketers

What today's AI tools can do across text, images, video and audio, where they genuinely help marketers, and why your judgement matters more than ever.
In this chapter you will learn to
  • Explain how AI tools generate content
  • Pick the right tool type for a task
  • Spot generic AI-sounding output

Lesson 1.1What AI tools are, in plain terms

AI chat tools such as ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Microsoft Copilot are built on large language models. They have learned patterns from a huge amount of text and predict what words should come next. That is why they write fluent captions, outlines and emails in seconds.

The same idea powers other tools: image generators create pictures from descriptions, and video and voice tools generate clips, edits and voiceovers. Together they can produce a draft of almost any marketing asset.

What this means for marketers

  1. Speed is no longer the edge. Everyone can now produce content quickly. Your advantage is insight, taste and knowing your customer.
  2. Average in, average out. A vague prompt gives the most typical answer, and typical is forgettable. Specific input gives distinctive output.
  3. It can be wrong. AI tools can invent statistics, quotes, product features and trends. Every claim you publish must be checked.
Tip

Think of AI as a tireless junior copywriter and researcher. Great at volume and first drafts. You are still the creative director.

Illustrative example

A two-person marketing team at a growing skincare brand used to publish three posts a week. They now hold one 45-minute planning session with AI each Monday, produce a week of drafts in an hour and spend the saved time filming real customer videos. Their engagement rose, and the reason was not the AI copy. It was the real content they finally had time to make.

The real opportunity

AI saves the most time on first drafts, repurposing, research summaries and variations. Use that time for things AI cannot do: talking to customers, building partnerships, creating original content and making sharp creative decisions.

Lesson 1.3Avoiding the generic AI voice

Audiences are learning to recognise AI-written content, and it costs trust. Common signs:

  • Openers like "In today's fast-paced world…" or "Are you ready to take your X to the next level?"
  • Words such as "unlock", "elevate", "seamless", "game-changer", "dive in"
  • Lists of three adjectives, too many emojis, and every sentence the same length
  • Claims with no specifics: "high quality", "best in class", "trusted by many"
Strip out the AI voice
Edit the text below so it sounds like a real person from our brand wrote it.
Remove clichés (e.g. "unlock", "elevate", "game-changer", "in today's world"), cut filler, vary sentence length, and replace vague claims with specific ones, using only the facts I give you.
If a claim has no supporting fact, mark it [NEEDS PROOF].

Facts about our product: [list]
Text: [paste]
Watch out

Never publish a statistic, award, customer number or quote that AI produced unless you can trace it to a real source. Invented "facts" are the fastest way to lose credibility.

  2. Run the starter prompt with your list.
  3. Challenge the result. Move anything where AI would be the only source of facts or creativity to the risky column.
  4. Pick three tasks to try during this course and note them.

Know your customer with AI

Use AI to turn reviews, comments, surveys and interviews into clear customer insight, and build personas grounded in real data, not guesswork.
In this chapter you will learn to

  • Build data-based personas

Lesson 2.1Mining what customers already say

Your customers are already telling you what they want, in reviews, comments, support messages, sales call notes and survey answers. Reading hundreds of them takes days. AI can summarise them in minutes, and you check the conclusions against the raw comments.

Review and comment analysis
Below are [number] customer reviews and comments about [product/service] (names removed).
Analyse them and give me:
1. The top 5 things customers value, each with 2 short example quotes copied exactly from the text.
2. The top 5 complaints or frustrations, with example quotes.
3. The exact words customers use to describe the problem we solve (useful for ad copy).
4. Any surprising or unexpected themes.
Only use what is in the text. Do not invent quotes.

[paste comments]
Tip

Point 3, the customer's own words, is gold. The phrases real customers use usually make better headlines than anything a copywriter or an AI invents.

Watch out

Remove names, phone numbers, emails and order numbers before pasting customer data into an AI tool, and follow your organisation's data policy and local data protection law.

Lesson 2.2Personas grounded in real data

AI will happily invent a persona called "Busy Sarah, 34, loves yoga". That is fiction. A useful persona comes from your data: interviews, sales notes, analytics and reviews.

Build a data-based persona
Using only the research notes below, build up to 3 customer personas.
For each: who they are (role, situation), main goal, biggest frustration, what triggers them to look for a solution, objections before buying, where they look for information, and 3 exact phrases from the notes that capture their voice.
If the data does not support a detail, write "unknown, needs research" rather than guessing.

[paste research notes, survey summaries, sales notes]
Illustrative example

A training company assumed its buyers were HR managers. Analysis of 60 enquiry emails showed most first contact came from team leaders who had a specific problem and then convinced HR. The company rewrote its ads for team leaders and halved its cost per enquiry.

Competitor scans

AI can help structure a competitor review: what they promise, who they target, how they price, what their reviews praise and criticise. Paste in what you collected from their websites and reviews. Do not rely on the AI's memory of a competitor, which may be outdated or wrong.

Lesson 2.3Better surveys and interviews

Bad questions give useless answers. AI is good at spotting leading or confusing questions and suggesting better ones.

Improve a survey
Review my customer survey below.
Flag questions that are leading, double-barrelled, confusing or likely to give useless answers, and suggest a better version of each.
Keep it under [number] questions and under 5 minutes to complete. Suggest the best order.

[paste survey]
Customer interview guide
Create a 20-minute customer interview guide to understand why customers chose [product] and what nearly stopped them.
Use open questions about past behaviour (what they did), not hypotheticals (what they would do).
Include follow-up probes and a short opening script.
Tip

After interviews, paste your notes (anonymised) and ask the AI to compare answers across customers: "Where do these 8 interviews agree, and where do they differ?"

Apply it at work

Find your customers' words

Turn real customer feedback into a short insight summary you can use in your next campaign.

  1. Collect 30 to 100 real reviews, comments or survey answers. Remove names and contact details.
  2. Run the starter prompt.
  3. Check 5 of the quotes the AI used against the original text to make sure they are real.
  4. Write down 3 customer phrases you will use in upcoming content.

Prompting and brand voice

A simple prompt structure for marketing, and a brand voice guide that makes AI drafts sound like you, not like everyone else.
In this chapter you will learn to

Lesson 3.1The RCTFC prompt structure

PartQuestion it answersExample
RoleWho should the AI act as?"Act as a senior copywriter for a premium coffee brand…"
ContextWhat is the situation?"…we are launching a cold brew to office workers aged 25 to 40…"
TaskWhat exactly should it produce?"…write 5 Instagram captions for launch week…"
FormatWhat should it look like?"…under 40 words each, one emoji max, end with a question…"
ChecksWhat rules and limits apply?"…use only the product facts below; no health claims; avoid the words 'elevate' and 'unlock'."
Launch captions (RCTFC)
Role: Act as a senior social media copywriter.
Context: We are [brand], selling [product] to [audience]. Key facts: [3 to 5 facts]. Our voice: [3 words, e.g. warm, witty, direct].
Task: Write 5 Instagram captions for launch week, each with a different angle (problem, benefit, behind the scenes, customer, offer).
Format: Under 40 words each, one emoji max, end with a question or a clear call to action.
Checks: Use only the facts given. No claims we cannot prove. Avoid: "elevate", "unlock", "game-changer".
Tip

Ask for options with different angles, not just "5 captions". Otherwise you get five versions of the same idea.

Lesson 3.2A brand voice guide for AI

The single best way to make AI sound like your brand is to give it a short voice guide and real examples every time. Examples beat descriptions.

What goes in the guide

  • Who we are in two sentences
  • Voice in 3 words, each with "this, not that" (for example: confident, not arrogant)
  • Words we use and words we never use
  • Rules: sentence length, emoji use, how we address the reader, spelling style
  • 3 to 5 examples of our best real posts or emails
Create a voice guide from examples
Below are 6 pieces of content our audience responded well to.
Analyse them and write a one-page brand voice guide: voice in 3 words (with "this, not that"), typical sentence length, vocabulary we use, words to avoid, how we open and close, emoji and punctuation habits.
Then write one new short post in this voice about [topic] so I can test the guide.

[paste examples]
Illustrative example

Save the guide in a document and paste it at the top of every content prompt, or save it in your AI tool's custom instructions or project settings if it has them. Consistency comes from using the same guide every time.

Lesson 3.3Iterating and adapting tone

Treat every first draft as a draft. Short, specific follow-ups improve it quickly:

  • Sharper: "Make the first line stop the scroll. Give me 5 opening lines only."
  • Different reader: "Rewrite for a busy finance director, not a consumer."
  • Different channel: "Turn this into a LinkedIn post, then a 15-second video script."
  • Critique: "Score this from 1 to 10 for clarity and persuasion, and explain how to reach 9."
  • Keep and fix: "Keep everything except the ending, which is too salesy."
Ask before writing
Before you write anything, ask me up to 5 questions about the audience, goal, offer and tone that you would need to do this really well. Wait for my answers.

Task: [describe the content]
Same message, many readers
Rewrite the message below for 3 audiences: [audience 1], [audience 2], [audience 3].
Keep the core offer identical. Change the angle, examples and tone for each. Under 80 words each.

[paste message]
Watch out

If a long chat starts producing worse results, start a fresh chat and paste in your voice guide and a complete prompt.

Apply it at work

Write your brand voice guide

Create a voice guide you will paste into every content prompt from now on.

  1. Collect 5 to 8 of your best-performing real posts, emails or pages.
  2. Run the starter prompt to draft the voice guide.
  3. Edit it: correct anything that does not feel like your brand and add your banned words.
  4. Test it: write one post with the guide and one without, and compare them.

Lesson 4.1Pillars and calendars

Content pillars are the 3 to 5 themes your brand talks about consistently. They keep content focused and make planning easy.

Content pillars
We are [brand], helping [audience] with [problem]. Our goals this quarter: [goals].
Suggest 4 content pillars. For each: why it matters to our audience, 5 post ideas, and which format suits it best (carousel, short video, story, article, email).
Base ideas on these customer insights: [paste insights from Module 2].
Monthly calendar
Using these pillars [paste], create a content calendar for [month] for [channels].
Posting frequency: [e.g. 3 per week on Instagram, 2 on LinkedIn, 1 email].
Include key dates relevant to our audience and industry: [list any you know].
Columns: date, channel, pillar, idea, format, call to action, owner.
Balance the pillars evenly and avoid repeating the same format back to back.
Watch out

AI may suggest "awareness days" or holidays that do not exist or fall on different dates. Check every date before you schedule anything.

Lesson 4.2One idea, ten pieces

Repurposing is where AI saves the most time. Start from one strong piece of original content (a webinar, an interview, a report, a case study) and turn it into many.

Repurpose a long piece

Turn it into:
- 5 short social posts, each built on one specific insight
- 1 LinkedIn article outline
- 3 short video ideas (hook, main point, call to action)
- 1 email newsletter section (under 150 words)
- 5 quote cards: exact quotes from the transcript, under 20 words each
Use only what is in the transcript. Keep our voice: [paste voice guide].

[paste transcript]
Illustrative example

A 40-minute customer panel recording became a month of content: 12 posts, 4 short clips, 2 emails and a blog post. The team spent one afternoon editing instead of two weeks writing from scratch.

Tip

Most video and meeting tools can export a transcript automatically. That transcript is your best raw material.

Lesson 4.3Blogs, SEO basics and video scripts

Blog posts that are worth reading

Search engines and readers reward content that is genuinely helpful and shows real experience. Use AI for the structure and first draft, then add what only you have: real examples, data, opinions and expertise.

Blog outline
Create an outline for a blog post answering: "[the question your customer types into a search engine]".
Audience: [who]. Goal: [what they should do after reading].
Include: a working title, the questions a reader would want answered (as H2 headings), where to add our own examples or data [ADD OUR EXAMPLE], and a short meta description under 155 characters.

Short video scripts

15 to 30 second video script
Write 3 short video scripts (15 to 30 seconds) about [topic] for [platform].
Each script: a hook in the first 2 seconds, the main point, and a call to action.
Show it as a table: time, what we see on screen, what is said, on-screen text.
It should be filmable on a phone by one person.
Watch out

Publishing large volumes of unedited AI content can hurt your brand and your search rankings. Fewer, better pieces beat many generic ones.

Lesson 5.3WhatsApp and messaging campaigns

In many markets, messaging apps like WhatsApp are where customers actually talk to businesses. Tools such as WhatsApp Business offer catalogues, labels, quick replies and broadcast features, and larger businesses use the WhatsApp Business Platform through approved providers.

Consent first

Messaging is personal. Only message people who have agreed to hear from you, make it easy to opt out, and do not send too often. WhatsApp's own business policies require opt-in, and many countries' laws regulate marketing messages. Spammy messaging gets numbers blocked and reported.

Lesson 6.1Briefing image tools

Image tools respond to clear, visual descriptions. A good image prompt covers subject, setting, style, lighting, composition and mood.

Image prompt structure
[Subject]: a ceramic coffee cup with steam rising, on a wooden desk next to an open laptop
[Setting]: bright modern office, morning
[Style]: clean product photography, natural colours
[Lighting]: soft window light from the left
[Composition]: close-up, cup on the left third, blurred background, empty space on the right for text
[Mood]: calm, focused
[Format]: square, 1:1
Let the chat tool write the image prompt
I need an image for [purpose, e.g. an Instagram post announcing our new opening hours].
Our brand colours: [colours]. Our style: [e.g. bright, minimal, real people, warm].
Write 3 detailed image-generation prompts in different styles, each with subject, setting, style, lighting, composition, mood and aspect ratio. Leave space for text.
Tip

Image tools are still unreliable with text inside images, hands and exact product details. Add text in a design tool like Canva afterwards, and use real photos for your actual products.

Lesson 6.2Staying on-brand

AI visuals can make every brand look the same. Keep yours distinct:

  • Use real photos where it matters most: your team, your products, your customers. Authentic images usually build more trust.
  • Use AI for the supporting parts: backgrounds, concepts, mock-ups, removing distractions, extending an image.
  • Create a visual style guide: colours, fonts, photo style, what to avoid. Paste the style parts into image prompts.
  • Use templates in your design tool so layouts stay consistent.
Visual style guide
Based on our brand description and these colours, draft a one-page visual style guide for social media: colour use, photo style (what to show and avoid), illustration style, text on images, and 5 example image prompts that follow the guide.

Brand: [description]
Colours: [hex codes]
Audience: [who]
Illustrative example

A fashion retailer uses AI to create seasonal backgrounds and scenes, but always photographs the actual clothes. Customers see exactly what they will receive, and returns stay low.

Lesson 6.3Rights, disclosure and trust

RuleWhy it matters
Never create realistic images or videos of real people without their permissionDeepfakes damage trust and may be illegal
Do not ask for images "in the style of" a living artist or copy brands, logos or charactersCopyright, trademark and reputation risks
Do not show AI images as real product photos or real resultsMisleading advertising rules in many countries
Follow platform rules on labelling AI contentMeta, TikTok and YouTube have AI-content labelling policies

"Before and after" images, testimonials with faces, and anything showing results (health, beauty, finance) must be real. Using AI here can break advertising law and destroy trust.

Tip

When in doubt, disclose. A simple note such as "Image created with AI" rarely hurts performance, and it protects your credibility.

Lesson 7.1The campaign brief

A clear brief makes every later step faster, for you, your team, your agency and the AI.

Campaign brief
Help me write a one-page campaign brief. Ask me questions first if anything is missing.
Sections: objective (one measurable goal), audience (from our research), key insight, single-minded message, proof points, offer, channels, budget, timeline, KPIs, and mandatory elements (logo, legal lines).
Here is what I know so far: [paste notes]
Stress-test the idea
Here is our campaign brief. Act as a sceptical marketing director.
List the 5 weakest points: unclear goals, weak insight, message not believable, wrong channel, unrealistic budget or timeline. For each, suggest a fix.

[paste brief]
Tip

"Single-minded message" means one main idea. If the brief has three messages, the campaign will communicate none of them well.

Lesson 7.2Ad copy and testing

AI is excellent at producing many genuinely different variations, which is exactly what testing needs.

Ad variations
Write ad copy for [platform] promoting [offer] to [audience].
Give me 6 variations, each using a different angle: pain point, benefit, social proof (use only these real facts: [facts]), urgency, curiosity, and a direct offer.
For each: headline (under [x] characters), primary text (under [x] characters), call to action.
Mark which 2 you would test first and why.

Test properly

  • Change one thing at a time (headline, image or offer) so you know what made the difference.
  • Give each test enough budget and time to reach a meaningful number of results before deciding.
  • Record what you learned in a simple test log, so you do not repeat tests.
Watch out

Ads in sensitive categories (health, finance, employment, housing, politics) have extra platform rules and legal requirements. Check them before you write, and never let AI add claims you cannot prove.

Lesson 8.2Ethics and the law

Trust is a marketer's most valuable asset. AI makes it easier to cut corners, so the rules matter more.

NeverAlways
Write fake reviews, testimonials or endorsementsUse real customer words, with permission
Invent statistics, awards or resultsCheck every claim against a source you can show
Paste customer personal data into tools without approvalAnonymise and follow data protection law
Copy competitors' or creators' workCreate original work and respect copyright
Hide paid partnerships or mislead with AI imagesDisclose sponsorships and label AI content where required
Target vulnerable groups with manipulative messagesMarket in a way you would be proud to explain publicly

Consumer protection and advertising regulators in many countries actively act against fake reviews and misleading claims, and data protection laws such as the GDPR in the EU and UK, and similar national laws elsewhere, regulate how you use customer data. Ask your legal or compliance contact when unsure.
